# Modified Kneser–Ney Smoothing

This note explains **Modified Kneser–Ney smoothing** from first principles and builds it on the ordinary Kneser–Ney idea.

The short version is:

> Kneser–Ney chooses the right lower-order distribution by counting distinct continuation contexts. Modified Kneser–Ney additionally uses different discount values for n-grams seen once, twice, and three or more times.

So there are two separate ideas:

```text
Kneser–Ney:       use continuation counts in the lower-order model
Modified Kneser–Ney: use continuation counts + count-dependent discounts
```

---

## 1. Why do we need another version of Kneser–Ney?

Suppose we are estimating a bigram model. We want to predict $w$ after history $h$:

$$
P(w\mid h)
$$

The maximum-likelihood estimate is:

$$
P_{ML}(w\mid h)=\frac{c(h,w)}{c(h)}
$$

If an n-gram has count zero, this estimate gives probability zero. Smoothing fixes that by moving some probability mass from observed n-grams to unseen n-grams.

Absolute discounting subtracts one fixed discount $D$ from every seen n-gram:

$$
P_{AD}(w\mid h)
=
\frac{\max(c(h,w)-D,0)}{c(h)}
+
\lambda(h)P_{lower}(w)
$$

For every observed n-gram, the direct count is changed from:

$$
c(h,w)
$$

to:

$$
c(h,w)-D
$$

The method treats every count in the same way with respect to $D$: count 1 loses $D$, count 2 loses $D$, and count 10 also loses $D$.

That is convenient, but it may not be statistically ideal. An n-gram seen once is much less reliable than an n-gram seen ten times. We therefore want the amount discounted to depend on the observed count.

That is the “modified” part.

---

## 2. One fixed discount versus several discounts

### Ordinary absolute discounting

Absolute discounting uses one value:

$$
D
$$

For example, if $D=0.75$:

| Observed count | Discounted count |
|---:|---:|
| 1 | (1-0.75=0.25) |
| 2 | (2-0.75=1.25) |
| 10 | (10-0.75=9.25) |

### Modified discounting

Modified Kneser–Ney uses different discounts:

$$
D_1 \quad \text{for count 1}
$$

$$
D_2 \quad \text{for count 2}
$$

$$
D_{3+} \quad \text{for count 3 or greater}
$$

For example, suppose:

$$
D_1=0.55,
\qquad
D_2=1.17,
\qquad
D_{3+}=1.89
$$

Then:

| Observed count | Discount used | Discounted count |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $D_1=0.55$ | $1-0.55=0.45$ |
| 2 | $D_2=1.17$ | $2-1.17=0.83$ |
| 10 | $D_{3+}=1.89$ | $10-1.89=8.11$ |

The discount is no longer a single blunt instrument. It has different strengths for different count classes.

---

## 3. The modified Kneser–Ney formula

For a history $h$ and candidate next word $w$, the interpolated Modified Kneser–Ney formula is:

$$
P_{MKN}(w\mid h)
=
\frac{\max(c(h,w)-D(c(h,w)),0)}{c(h)}
+
\lambda(h)P_{MKN}(w\mid h')
$$

Here:

- $h$ is the current, longer history;
- $h'$ is the shorter history obtained by removing the oldest word from $h$;
- $c(h,w)$ is the observed count of the current n-gram;
- $c(h)$ is the count of the history;
- $D(c(h,w))$ chooses $D_1$, $D_2$, or $D_{3+}$;
- $\lambda(h)$ is the backoff/interpolation weight;
- $P_{MKN}(w\mid h')$ is the lower-order Modified Kneser–Ney probability.

The count-dependent discount is:

$$
D(c)=
\begin{cases}
D_1 & \text{if }c=1\\[4pt]
D_2 & \text{if }c=2\\[4pt]
D_{3+} & \text{if }c\ge 3
\end{cases}
$$

The first term handles evidence from the current history. The second term gives the word probability inherited from the shorter history.

The model is interpolated because the lower-order term can contribute even when $c(h,w)>0$.

---

## 4. The most important comparison

The ordinary Kneser–Ney formula usually has one discount $D$:

$$
P_{KN}(w\mid h)
=
\frac{\max(c(h,w)-D,0)}{c(h)}
+
\lambda(h)P_{KN}(w\mid h')
$$

Modified Kneser–Ney changes only the discounting part:

$$
\max(c(h,w)-D,0)
$$

becomes:

$$
\max(c(h,w)-D(c(h,w)),0)
$$

The lower-order continuation idea remains Kneser–Ney’s central idea.

```text
Ordinary KN:       one D + continuation probabilities
Modified KN:       D₁/D₂/D₃₊ + continuation probabilities
```

This distinction is worth remembering: **modified** refers primarily to the discount schedule, not to replacing the continuation distribution with an ordinary unigram distribution.

---

## 5. Computing the interpolation weight $\lambda(h)$

Discounting removes probability mass from the observed continuations after $h$. The interpolation weight must equal exactly the total removed mass divided by the history count.

Let:

- $N_1(h,\cdot)$ be the number of words $w$ such that $c(h,w)=1$;
- $N_2(h,\cdot)$ be the number of words $w$ such that $c(h,w)=2$;
- $N_{3+}(h,\cdot)$ be the number of words $w$ such that $c(h,w)\ge3$.

Then:

$$
\lambda(h)
=
\frac{
D_1N_1(h,\cdot)
+D_2N_2(h,\cdot)
+D_{3+}N_{3+}(h,\cdot)
}{c(h)}
$$

Why does this formula make sense?

For every continuation seen once, we remove $D_1$ count units. There are $N_1(h,\cdot)$ such continuations, so the total removed from count-one continuations is:

$$
D_1N_1(h,\cdot)
$$

Likewise, the total removed from count-two continuations is:

$$
D_2N_2(h,\cdot)
$$

And the total removed from count-three-or-more continuations is:

$$
D_{3+}N_{3+}(h,\cdot)
$$

Adding those gives the total amount removed from the history. Dividing by $c(h)$ turns removed count into removed probability mass.

## 6. Complete numerical example of the higher-order part

Suppose the history is:

```text
San ___
```

The training data gives these continuation counts:

```text
San Francisco       10 times
San Jose              2 times
San Diego             1 time
San Mateo             1 time
```

Therefore:

$$
c(\text{San})=10+2+1+1=14
$$

The continuation-count buckets are:

$$
N_1(\text{San},\cdot)=2
$$

because `Diego` and `Mateo` each occur once;

$$
N_2(\text{San},\cdot)=1
$$

because `Jose` occurs twice; and:

$$
N_{3+}(\text{San},\cdot)=1
$$

because `Francisco` occurs ten times.

Use the illustrative discounts:

$$
D_1=0.5556,
\qquad
D_2=1.1667,
\qquad
D_{3+}=1.8889
$$

### 6.1 Discount each observed continuation

For `Francisco`, whose count is 10, use $D_{3+}$:

$$
c(\text{San},\text{Francisco})-D_{3+}
=10-1.8889
=8.1111
$$

Its direct probability contribution is:

$$
\frac{8.1111}{14}
\approx0.5794
$$

For `Jose`, whose count is 2, use $D_2$:

$$
c(\text{San},\text{Jose})-D_2
=2-1.1667
=0.8333
$$

Its direct probability contribution is:

$$
\frac{0.8333}{14}
\approx0.0595
$$

For `Diego`, whose count is 1, use $D_1$:

$$
c(\text{San},\text{Diego})-D_1
=1-0.5556
=0.4444
$$

Its direct probability contribution is:

$$
\frac{0.4444}{14}
\approx0.0317
$$

`Mateo` has the same count and therefore the same direct contribution:

$$
\frac{0.4444}{14}\approx0.0317
$$

### 6.2 Compute the removed mass

The total discounted count removed is:

$$
(2\times0.5556)+(1\times1.1667)+(1\times1.8889)
$$

Calculate each part:

$$
2\times0.5556=1.1112
$$

$$
1\times1.1667=1.1667
$$

$$
1\times1.8889=1.8889
$$

Therefore:

$$
1.1112+1.1667+1.8889=4.1668
$$

The interpolation weight is:

$$
\lambda(\text{San})=\frac{4.1668}{14}\approx0.2976
$$

The direct contributions sum to approximately:

$$
0.5794+0.0595+0.0317+0.0317=0.7023
$$

Adding the removed mass gives:

$$
0.7023+0.2976\approx0.9999\approx1
$$

The tiny difference from 1 is only rounding.

This is the normalization story in one complete calculation:

```text
direct probability mass kept = about 0.7023
probability mass redistributed = about 0.2976
total = about 1.0000
```

## 7. What happens for an unseen continuation?

Now ask for:

```text
San university
```

Suppose:

$$
c(\text{San},\text{university})=0
$$

Then the direct term is:

$$
\frac{\max(0-D(0),0)}{c(\text{San})}=0
$$

There is no discount to select for count zero because the n-gram has no direct count. The probability comes from the lower-order model:

$$
P_{MKN}(\text{university}\mid\text{San})
=
\lambda(\text{San})
P_{MKN}(\text{university})
$$

Using the value calculated above:

$$
P_{MKN}(\text{university}\mid\text{San})
\approx0.2976\times P_{MKN}(\text{university})
$$

The lower-order probability is not an ordinary token-frequency unigram. It is a continuation probability:

$$
P_{MKN}(w)
=
\frac{N_{1+}(\cdot,w)}{N_{1+}(\cdot,\cdot)}
$$

This is where the two Kneser–Ney ideas meet:

1. The current history uses count-dependent discounting.
2. The lower-order model uses the number of distinct contexts that can lead to the word.

---

## 8. Recursion: Modified Kneser–Ney for trigrams

The bigram example is only one level. For a trigram, let the history be two words:

```text
I visited San ___
```

The current history is:

$$
h=(\text{visited},\text{San})
$$

The modified Kneser–Ney equation is:

$$
P_{MKN}(w\mid\text{visited},\text{San})
=
\frac{\max(c(\text{visited},\text{San},w)-D(c(\text{visited},\text{San},w)),0)}{c(\text{visited},\text{San})}
+
\lambda(\text{visited},\text{San})
P_{MKN}(w\mid\text{San})
$$

If `I visited San Francisco` has been observed many times, the trigram term directly models that precise context. If the trigram is unseen, the model falls back to the bigram context `San`.

Then the bigram model may itself use the unigram continuation distribution:

$$
P_{MKN}(w\mid\text{San})
\rightarrow
P_{MKN}(w)
$$

The complete path is:

```text
trigram:       P(w | visited, San)
                   ↓ if needed
bigram:        P(w | San)
                   ↓ if needed
continuation:  P(w)
```

At every higher order, Modified Kneser–Ney repeats the same two operations:

1. Discount the observed count using the appropriate count bucket.
2. Pass the removed mass to the shorter-history model.

## 9. Where do $D_1$, $D_2$, and $D_{3+}$ come from?

The discounts are estimated from **frequency-of-frequencies** statistics.

Let $n_r$ mean:

> the number of distinct n-gram types whose count is exactly $r$.

For example:

```text
n₁ = number of n-gram types seen exactly once
n₂ = number of n-gram types seen exactly twice
n₃ = number of n-gram types seen exactly three times
n₄ = number of n-gram types seen exactly four times
```

The standard Modified Kneser–Ney discounts use:

$$
Y=\frac{n_1}{n_1+2n_2}
$$

Then:

$$
D_1=1-2Y\frac{n_2}{n_1}
$$

$$
D_2=2-3Y\frac{n_3}{n_2}
$$

$$
D_{3+}=3-4Y\frac{n_4}{n_3}
$$

These formulas estimate how much count mass should be removed from each count class.

The exact notation can vary slightly across papers and implementations, but the structure is the same:

```text
estimate a discount for count 1
estimate a discount for count 2
estimate a discount for count 3 or more
```

The discounts are usually estimated separately for each n-gram order in a full implementation. That means bigrams and trigrams may have different $D_1$, $D_2$, and $D_{3+}$ values.

---

## 10. Calculate the discount values from frequency-of-frequencies

Use this illustrative dataset:

$$
n_1=10,
\qquad
n_2=4,
\qquad
n_3=2,
\qquad
n_4=1
$$

### 10.1 Compute $Y$

$$
Y=\frac{n_1}{n_1+2n_2}
=\frac{10}{10+2(4)}
=\frac{10}{18}
\approx0.5556
$$

### 10.2 Compute $D_1$

$$
D_1=1-2Y\frac{n_2}{n_1}
$$

Substitute the numbers:

$$
D_1
=1-2(0.5556)\frac{4}{10}
$$

$$
2(0.5556)\frac{4}{10}
=1.1112\times0.4
=0.4445
$$

Therefore:

$$
D_1\approx1-0.4445=0.5555
$$

Rounded consistently, this is about (0.5556).

### 10.3 Compute $D_2$

$$
D_2=2-3Y\frac{n_3}{n_2}
$$

Substitute:

$$
D_2
=2-3(0.5556)\frac{2}{4}
$$

$$
3(0.5556)\frac{2}{4}
=1.6668\times0.5
=0.8334
$$

Therefore:

$$
D_2\approx2-0.8334=1.1666
$$

So $D_2\approx1.1667$.

### 10.4 Compute $D_{3+}$

$$
D_{3+}=3-4Y\frac{n_4}{n_3}
$$

Substitute:

$$
D_{3+}
=3-4(0.5556)\frac{1}{2}
$$

$$
4(0.5556)\frac{1}{2}
=2.2224\times0.5
=1.1112
$$

Therefore:

$$
D_{3+}\approx3-1.1112=1.8888
$$

So $D_{3+}\approx1.8889$.

These are the illustrative values used in the earlier numerical example.

## 11. Why are the discounts different?

Consider the three count classes.

### Count 1

An n-gram seen once is weak evidence. It may be a rare accident or a sample-specific event. Modified Kneser–Ney uses $D_1$ to remove an amount calibrated specifically for singleton types.

### Count 2

An n-gram seen twice has more evidence than a singleton, but it is still sparse. Its discount $D_2$ is estimated separately.

### Count 3 or more

An n-gram seen at least three times has a different statistical behavior. Rather than assigning a separate parameter to every possible count, the usual formulation groups all counts (3,4,5,\ldots) into one class and uses $D_{3+}$.

The method is not claiming that every count-ten n-gram is equally reliable. It is using a practical three-parameter approximation:

```text
one discount for count 1
one discount for count 2
one discount for count 3 and above
```

## 12. An intuitive analogy: different tax brackets

Imagine discounting as a tax on observed n-gram evidence.

Absolute discounting uses one flat tax:

```text
Every observed n-gram loses D count units.
```

Modified Kneser–Ney uses count brackets:

```text
count-1 bracket:      tax D₁
count-2 bracket:      tax D₂
count-3-or-more:      tax D₃₊
```

The purpose is not to punish every n-gram identically. The purpose is to estimate how much evidence in each count bracket should be reserved for unseen events.

The analogy maps to the equations like this:

| Analogy | Modified Kneser–Ney quantity |
|---|---|
| Evidence before tax | $c(h,w)$ |
| Tax amount | $D_1$, $D_2$, or $D_{3+}$ |
| Evidence kept for this history | $c(h,w)-D(c(h,w))$ |
| Tax collected from all continuations | numerator of $\lambda(h)$ |
| Tax redistributed | lower-order Kneser–Ney probability |

This is why the interpolation weight is computed from the sum of discounts removed across all observed continuation types.

## 13. Modified Kneser–Ney versus ordinary Kneser–Ney

| Property | Ordinary Kneser–Ney | Modified Kneser–Ney |
|---|---|---|
| Lower-order distribution | Continuation probability | Continuation probability |
| Discount for count 1 | One shared $D$ | $D_1$ |
| Discount for count 2 | Same shared $D$ | $D_2$ |
| Discount for count 3+ | Same shared $D$ | $D_{3+}$ |
| Interpolation weight | Based on one discount per observed type | Based on the appropriate discount per count class |
| Main benefit | Correctly models contextual continuation | Correct continuation model plus more flexible discounting |

The lower-order probability is still the crucial Kneser–Ney component:

$$
P_{cont}(w)
=
\frac{N_{1+}(\cdot,w)}{N_{1+}(\cdot,\cdot)}
$$

The modification improves how probability mass is removed before it is passed to that continuation model.

---

## 14. Modified Kneser–Ney versus absolute discounting

| Question | Absolute discounting | Modified Kneser–Ney |
|---|---|---|
| How is a seen count discounted? | Subtract one $D$ | Subtract $D_1$, $D_2$, or $D_{3+}$ |
| What does the lower-order model count? | Usually token frequency | Distinct continuation contexts |
| Does it distinguish count classes? | No | Yes |
| Does it recurse to shorter histories? | In interpolated/backoff versions, yes | Yes |
| Main failure it addresses | Zero probabilities and sparse counts | Poor lower-order distribution plus overly rigid discounting |

The two improvements address different weaknesses:

```text
Kneser–Ney improvement:   fix what “unigram probability” means.
Modified improvement:     fix how much to discount different counts.
```

## 15. A full recursive mental model

When predicting a word from a long history, Modified Kneser–Ney asks:

### At the current order

```text
Have I seen this n-gram?
```

If yes:

1. Look at its count.
2. Select the correct discount bucket.
3. Keep the discounted direct contribution.
4. Pass the removed mass to the shorter-history model.

If no:

1. The direct contribution is zero.
2. Pass the available probability through the lower-order model.

### At the next order

Repeat the same process with the shorter history.

```text
long history
    ↓ count-dependent discount
shorter history
    ↓ count-dependent discount
even shorter history
    ↓
continuation probability based on distinct contexts
```

The method therefore combines three intuitions:

1. **Specific evidence:** longer histories model precise phrases.
2. **Count reliability:** different observed counts deserve different discounts.
3. **Contextual versatility:** the final lower-order probability depends on how many different contexts can lead to a word.

---

## 16. Final takeaway

The complete Modified Kneser–Ney story is:

> Discount every observed n-gram, but do not use one discount blindly. Use $D_1$ for singleton counts, $D_2$ for count-two n-grams, and $D_{3+}$ for larger counts. Redistribute the removed probability mass recursively using Kneser–Ney continuation probabilities, where a word is judged by the number of different contexts that can precede it.

The compact formula to remember is:

$$
P_{MKN}(w\mid h)
=
\frac{\max(c(h,w)-D(c(h,w)),0)}{c(h)}
+
\lambda(h)P_{MKN}(w\mid h')
$$

with:

$$
D(c)=D_1,D_2,\text{ or }D_{3+}
$$

depending on whether $c$ is $1$, $2$, or at least $3$, and:

$$
\lambda(h)
=
\frac{
D_1N_1(h,\cdot)
+D_2N_2(h,\cdot)
+D_{3+}N_{3+}(h,\cdot)
}{c(h)}
$$

If you remember only one contrast, remember this:

```text
Kneser–Ney asks: What does the word’s continuation behavior look like?
Modified Kneser–Ney additionally asks: How reliable is this particular count class?
```
